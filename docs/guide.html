Product guide

WardBeat

WardBeat reads a hospital ward’s notes and shows who is medically fit to leave and what is blocking them, so beds free up sooner and more safely.

● live on NVIDIA NIM Next.js 16 · FastAPI · Postgres/pgvector
01

The problem

Wards lose bed capacity not because patients cannot leave, but because flow stalls and the reason is hard to see. The status that matters — who is medically fit, and what is blocking their discharge — lives in free-text notes, not structured fields. Finding it means re-reading notes and chasing people by hand.

It helps to name what kind of problem this actually is. A ward is rarely short of beds because it is short of beds; it is short of beds because it cannot see, at a glance, which of today’s patients are ready to go home and what small thing is holding each of them up. That is an information problem wearing the costume of a capacity problem. The raw facts exist — a nurse wrote “medically fit, awaiting pharmacy TTOs” at 9am — but they exist as a sentence inside one note among hundreds, never rolled up into a picture anyone can act on.

Signal buried in notes

Discharge status, the expected date, and every barrier sit in free-text nursing and ward-round notes.

Invisible barriers

A patient is fit to go but stuck on medications, transport, social care, or a review, with no single view of why.

Reactive coordination

Teams firefight in the afternoon. Board rounds are assembled by hand, and discharges slip past noon.

Why it matters The cost of the blind spot is measured in bed-days. A patient who was fit at 9am but whose discharge is only spotted at the afternoon board round leaves at 4pm instead of before noon — and that bed sits empty, or worse, an admission waits in the emergency department for it. Multiply one slipped discharge by every ward, every day, and the invisible signal becomes very visible on the wait list. WardBeat’s whole reason to exist is to surface that signal early enough to act on it.

02

What WardBeat does

WardBeat reads every note on the ward and turns it into a live board: who is fit to leave, what is blocking each patient, and what to do next. Every answer is grounded in a real note or policy passage you can click through to.

It presents that work as four surfaces, each solving a different slice of the flow problem. They share one design principle, so it is worth stating up front: every surface is grounded and cited, and none of them acts on its own. WardBeat’s job is to compress hundreds of notes into a live picture and to suggest the next move — never to make the move. A clinician stays in the loop on every decision. That is what makes an AI system safe to put in front of a ward.

Copilot

Actions

Flow briefing

Why each surface looks the way it does

1

The board is a view, not a data-entry screen

The ward board is the picture the ward never had: one row per bed, each showing its barriers, a discharge probability, and an action badge, so the fit-but-delayed patients jump out. It is read-derived — the board is a projection of what the notes already say, not a second place to type things. That is deliberate: staff already have a record system, and asking them to maintain a second one is how tools die on the ward.

Why it matters The board earns its place by removing work, not adding it. Because it is computed from the notes rather than hand-filled, it is always current and never becomes another chore to keep in sync.

2

The copilot answers, but only from sources

The copilot lets anyone ask about the live ward (“who is medically fit but still here?”) or about discharge policy (“what’s the criteria for a supported discharge?”) in plain language. Ward questions become a structured, validated filter over the real data; policy questions retrieve the actual passages and answer from them, with citations. If it cannot ground an answer, it says it does not know rather than guessing.

Why it matters A chatbot that confidently makes something up is worse than no chatbot on a ward. Refusing to answer without a source, and always showing that source, is what lets a clinician trust — and verify — what it says.

3

Actions recommend; a person approves

For every barrier on the board, the actions agent proposes one concrete next step drawn from policy — chase the TTOs, book transport, refer to social care, request a review. It never fires the step itself. The coordinator sees the suggestion and its policy rationale, then approves the ones worth chasing, and every approval is audited.

Why it matters Suggesting the next step is genuinely useful and carries no risk; taking it automatically would cross the line into acting on a patient’s care. Keeping approval with a human is the line that keeps WardBeat firmly in decision-support territory.

4

The briefing separates the numbers from the words

The flow briefing is a short shift summary with the net bed position — how many discharges are likely, how many admissions are expected, where the ward nets out. Crucially, the numbers come from a plain statistical model, and the AI only writes the prose around them. The model does the maths; the language model does the language.

Why it matters Bed forecasts have to be calibrated and testable, which a language model’s free-form guess is not. By having the LLM narrate figures it was handed rather than invent them, the briefing reads naturally while every number stays accountable.

Decision-support, not autonomy. WardBeat recommends and drafts; clinicians decide. Every AI output is grounded and cited, no model takes a clinical action on its own, and the whole system runs on synthetic data with zero patient information.
03

Patient flow, end to end

One patient, from admission to a freed bed, and where WardBeat helps. WardBeat never moves a patient or sends an order. It reads the notes, makes the blockers visible, and suggests the next step; a clinician decides and acts. The shaded stages below are where WardBeat adds signal.

Reading the journey, notice where WardBeat sits. It does not touch the ends — admission and discharge stay entirely with clinicians and the systems of record. What it adds is in the middle: turning the running stream of notes into a visible status, and turning that status into a worklist, early enough that the barriers can be cleared before they cost a morning. The whole value is in moving the moment of discovery earlier, not in taking anyone’s decision away.

Admission

A patient takes a bed

The stay begins. In the data a new encounter opens on that bed and starts collecting notes.

Care & notes

The team records progress as free text

Nurses and the ward round write notes through the stay. This is the richest and messiest source of truth on the ward, and where the discharge status really lives.

Read & structure

WardBeat reads each note into data

Extraction turns the note into structured barriers, an estimated discharge date, and a medically-fit flag, each one grounded in the exact sentence it came from.

Board & barriers

The status becomes visible

The bed now shows its blockers, a discharge probability, and an action badge. The fit-but-delayed patients, the ones losing bed-days, stand out at a glance.

Chase actions

Barriers become a worklist

For each barrier WardBeat suggests the next step from policy; the coordinator approves the ones to chase, be it TTOs, transport, social care, or a review. Recommend-only, and every approval is audited.

Discharge

Fit, cleared, and gone by noon

Once the patient is medically fit and the barriers are cleared, they leave and the bed frees. Because the blockers surfaced early, this happens in the morning rather than at 4pm.

Why it matters The design choice worth dwelling on is that WardBeat surfaces the blockers rather than resolving them. It would be technically simpler in places to let a model auto-book transport or auto-refer to pharmacy — but a wrong automated action on a real patient is a clinical incident, while a wrong suggestion is just a suggestion a person declines. Surfacing-early keeps every ounce of the time saving and none of the risk: the human still holds the decision, they just hold it hours sooner and with the barrier already spelled out.

WardBeat assists; people act. It does not admit or discharge patients, and it does not message pharmacy or transport. Those stay with clinicians and the systems of record. Automating the admit and discharge steps so beds turn over on their own is on the roadmap; today WardBeat makes the flow visible and the barriers actionable.
04

Architecture

Four planes behind a single front door. Next.js is the only thing the browser talks to, and the only thing that writes to the database. The AI work runs in a small internal service that holds no state, so it is easy to scale, test, and move on-prem.

Why it matters The shape of this diagram is a safety argument, not just an engineering one. One front door means one place to enforce who can see what; one database writer means data can never drift out of sync; and a stateless AI plane that never touches the database means the model layer holds no patient data at rest and can be restarted, scaled, or swapped without risk. This overview sketches the shape — the Architecture, Azure, and Monitoring tabs go deep on the topology, the cloud deployment, and how it is observed in production.

Deployment

The whole stack runs from Docker. The hosted demo and an on-prem install use the same containers and the same OpenAI-compatible model contract, so moving into a hospital is a config change — swap the model URL and key — not a rewrite. That is what lets clinical data stay inside the trust.

05

AI tooling & models

The guiding rule is to use generative AI where it belongs and no further. Language models do the language: reading notes into structured data, answering questions from retrieved sources, and turning numbers into prose. Everything numeric or safety-critical stays in plain, testable code. The choices below follow from two constraints: clinical data should be able to stay inside the hospital, and quality has to be measurable.

Why it matters Every model decision here is really a trust decision. Choosing self-hostable models keeps patient data inside the trust; choosing the smallest model that does the job keeps the system fast enough to run on every note and cheap enough to run on-prem; and wrapping each call in validation means the model is treated as a fallible component, not an oracle. The AI tab expands each of these choices in depth, and the Evals tab shows how every one of them is held to a number.

NVIDIA NIM

Inference platform

Self-hostable inference microservices behind an OpenAI-compatible API. One vendor supplies the language, embedding, and rerank models the product needs.

  • Data sovereignty. The same containers run on the hospital’s own GPUs, so patient data never leaves the trust.
  • No lock-in. The API is OpenAI-compatible, so the client code is standard and the models can be swapped.
  • Demo to production is a config change. The hosted free tier runs the demo; production points the same code at on-prem NIM by swapping a base URL and key.

Instead of a hosted frontier API (no on-prem option, data leaves the trust) or raw self-hosting with vLLM (you rebuild serving, optimisation, embedding, and reranking yourself). NIM is the middle path.

The three models

Each model is the smallest one that does its job well. Nothing here needs a frontier model, and a smaller model is faster, cheaper, and easier to run on-prem.

The harness around the model

A prompt is the smallest part. Most of the reliability comes from the code wrapped around each model call.

Offline mock, one flag

Every capability has a deterministic offline stand-in. With no API key, or one flag flipped, the whole system runs with no model at all — and a failed live call falls back to the mock rather than breaking the app.

Rate limit & timeout

A token-bucket limiter keeps calls under the tier’s request budget, and every call is bounded by a timeout so a stalled model can never wedge a batch.

Structured output, then validate

Models are asked for strict JSON, and the result is re-checked in code against allow-lists. The model’s field names and values are never trusted as-is.

Grounded or nothing

Extracted claims are matched back to the source note; answers must cite a retrieved passage or refuse. Unverifiable output is dropped.

When not to use a language model. Discharge probability and bed demand come from a small, transparent statistical model with readable weights, not the LLM. Numbers have to be calibrated and testable; the LLM only narrates them, and is forbidden from inventing or changing a figure. Knowing where to stop using AI is part of the design.

Evaluation

AI quality here is a number, not a feeling. Each capability has its own harness with three parts: a labelled synthetic dataset, a metric that fits the task, and a hard gate. The harnesses run against the live NVIDIA NIM models and exit non-zero when a gate is missed, so they can act as a release gate. Below are the figures from the current live run.

Why it matters This is the part that turns “we tried to be careful” into “we can prove it.” In a clinical setting, an AI feature you cannot measure is an AI feature you cannot defend — so every capability is pinned to a metric and a gate, and nothing ships if a gate is missed. The Evals tab walks through each harness and dataset in full, and the Monitoring tab shows how the same signals are watched once the system is live, not just at release time.

The four harnesses

Harness What it checks Gate
Extraction
eval:extraction		 For each labelled note, the extracted barrier types are scored against the planted ground truth as precision, recall, and F1, plus fit-to-leave accuracy. F1 ≥ 0.85
Copilot
eval:copilot		 Policy path: does the vector search then rerank retrieve the expected document (hit-rate), and is the answer grounded? Ward path: does the question become the right structured filter (query-intent accuracy)? all ≥ 0.90
Actions
eval:actions		 For each labelled barrier, is the recommended action the appropriate one, and is its rationale grounded in a retrieved policy passage? both ≥ 0.90
Forecast & narration
eval:forecast		 Does the discharge model rank patients sensibly, measured by Spearman rank correlation against a truth score? And does the briefing state only numbers it was given (numeric-consistency), proving the narrator never invents figures? ρ ≥ 0.70; consistency ≥ 0.90
Measured, not guessed. The extraction harness earned its keep early: it caught the first model choice underperforming and let the prompt be tuned from a measured 69% to 88% F1. Without the number, that regression would have been invisible.

Why these metrics

F1 for extraction

Extraction can fail two ways — missing a real barrier or inventing one. F1 balances recall against precision, so neither is hidden.

Hit-rate & groundedness for RAG

Retrieval is only useful if the right passage comes back, and an answer is only safe if it is grounded in what was retrieved. Both are checked.

Rank correlation for the forecast

The forecast is used to order patients by dischargeability, so it is scored on getting the order right, not on hitting an exact probability.

Numeric-consistency for narration

The one failure that matters for a narrator is stating a wrong number. Checking that every figure came from the input catches exactly that.
